Where the Tenfold Goes

Semantic Entropy asks the model ten times, and the instinct is to brace for ten times the wait. That instinct is wrong, and understanding why tells you exactly what the tenfold does cost — and on which axis you'll actually feel it.

The bill lands on the wrong axis

Start with the correction, because everything after it is the mechanism that makes it true.

the instinct

Ten generations means ten times the latency. One user waits ten times as long as they would for a plain answer.

what actually happens

Ten generations means ten times the throughput cost, at close to unchanged latency for the one user. The same box now serves a fraction of the people it used to.

The ten sequences do not queue up behind each other. They are issued as a single request with n=10, and the server runs them together — one batch, advancing all ten a token at a time in lockstep. A GPU is enormously parallel and mostly idle during single-sequence generation; ten sequences fill capacity that was already sitting there.

So the user's wall-clock wait barely moves. What moves is how much of the machine that one request consumed — and therefore how many other users fit alongside it.

The mechanism — two phases, priced differently

Prefill is shared. Decode is not.

Generation is not one uniform activity. It splits into two phases with completely different cost structures, and the entire tenfold question turns on which phase your workload lives in.

One request, n=10, from prompt to ten answers

width ∝ compute
shared across all ten sequences multiplied by ten
Phase 1 · prefill

Reading the question

  • Every prompt token attends over every other — one dense, highly parallel pass
  • Produces the KV cache: the model's internal read of the prompt
  • All ten sequences continue from that same cache
  • Compute-bound; the GPU is genuinely busy here

cost with n=10 · unchanged

Phase 2 · decode

Writing the answers

  • One token at a time, per sequence, each appending to its own cache
  • Ten different continuations means ten separate token streams
  • Nothing is shared — this is where the multiplier is real
  • Memory-bandwidth-bound; batching is what makes it survivable

cost with n=10 · ×10

This is what prefix caching buys you, and it is worth being precise about the claim: the saving is not a clever optimisation you enable. It is structural. The ten sequences share a prompt, so they share its cache, so the prompt is read once no matter how many answers you ask for.

The consequence — how bad is it really

Your prompt-to-answer ratio sets the multiplier

Because one phase is shared and the other is not, the true cost of n=10 is not a fixed 10×. It slides with the shape of your workload — and the direction of the slide is favourable for exactly the workloads people actually run.

Workload shape Prompt Answer Cost at n=10 Concurrent users
RAG with retrieved context 2000 tok 40 tok ≈ 2.6× ≈ 38% of before
Long system prompt, short reply 800 tok 50 tok ≈ 4.4× ≈ 23% of before
Bare factual question 20 tok 15 tok ≈ 8.7× ≈ 11% of before
Open-ended generation 50 tok 600 tok ≈ 9.9× ≈ 10% of before

Illustrative arithmetic on total token-work, prefill shared and decode multiplied. Real numbers depend on model, hardware and server; measure your own.

The pattern is clean: the longer your prompt relative to your answer, the cheaper SE gets. A long shared prefix is a large fixed cost you now amortise over ten outputs instead of one.

Which lands awkwardly for the benchmark this method is usually evaluated on. TriviaQA is short question, short answer — the worst corner of the table. The literature's headline cost is close to the honest worst case, and a production RAG pipeline typically fares better than the paper implies.

The constraint that bites first

You may run out of memory before you run out of compute

Ten caches, not one

Each of the ten sequences accumulates its own KV cache as it decodes. The prompt's portion is shared, but every generated token adds per-sequence state — so VRAM use grows with the batch, and it grows during generation, not just at the start.

On a memory-constrained card this is the ceiling you hit first. The server will simply admit fewer concurrent requests, or preempt and requeue some, long before the arithmetic above becomes the binding constraint. On an L4 this is a real limit, not a footnote.

The practical symptom is not an error. It is throughput quietly falling short of what the compute numbers predicted, because the scheduler is holding requests back to fit the caches it already has in flight.

What this means for the choice

The tenfold is real, but it is a capacity cost, not a waiting cost. That distinction decides where SE can live.

Reading the tradeoff correctly

  1. Latency is not the objection. If someone rejects SE because "users would wait ten times longer," they have the wrong model of how batched inference works. Say so plainly — the wait is roughly unchanged.
  2. Capacity is the objection. The same hardware serves a fraction of the users. That is a real, budget-visible cost, and it is why SE belongs off the request path for most teams.
  3. Your ratio matters more than the number ten. Long-prompt workloads pay a fraction of the headline multiplier. Measure your own prompt-to-answer ratio before accepting 10× as your number.
  4. The NLI pass is a separate bill. Everything above concerns generation only. Clustering ten answers adds tens of small forward passes through a second model that must also be resident in VRAM.
  5. None of this is what the probe fixes. SEP removes the ten generations entirely — one forward pass, one dot product. The arithmetic here is the size of the problem it exists to delete.

The wait stays. The capacity is what you spend.
